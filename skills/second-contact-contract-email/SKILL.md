# Second-contact contract email

Write one developing thought: current work → a specific problem in their work → overlap with skills Andrew already uses → applying those skills to work with a real-world benefit → small contract project → one tentative idea → willingness to work on a more useful problem → call. Each paragraph must causally lead into the next, not merely satisfy the next section.

## Establish the relationship first

Use the parent [Andrew workflow](../andrew-email-style/SKILL.md) for research, Gmail history, Apollo, persistence and suppression. Read the actual earlier sent message. Confirm the first contact's name and date, then check whether anyone else at this company has already been contacted or replied. Do not create a third contact disguised as a second contact. Same-day drafting is permitted when explicitly requested; it does not authorize sending or a permanent cadence exception.

Choose a relevant manager, engineering/research lead, director, Head, CTO or founder who can plausibly sponsor or route a bounded project. Technical similarity alone is insufficient. Record evidence of their function and seniority separately from the hypothesis that they can authorize outside work. Do not assume budget authority, or choose an individual data scientist merely because their research matches. Retain sent history unchanged when targeting preferences change.

Read [approved-examples.md](references/approved-examples.md) before writing. Nathan, Steve and Jeaffrey are exact `contracts`-labelled messages Andrew sent, not generated rewrites or proven reply winners. The newer Sajjad and Seema examples in that reference take priority for the latest continuity and explicit alternative-work wording. The older examples remain historical cadence evidence; their recipient seniority is not the current targeting policy. Use existing company-specific thought as the seed. Do not invent a more impressive angle.

Before composing, research the active project, existing approach, relevant owner and a contained contribution. Read technical documentation, releases, research or relevant job descriptions, not just the homepage. Check whether they already do the proposed work. A source saying a problem was fixed is not evidence it remains unresolved. Save observed work, inferred possible need and unknown internal priority separately. Prefer a useful extension to their current work over offering to recreate it; public activity or funding never proves they will hire Andrew. Change the project idea only when this research gives a concrete reason, and keep the research mostly behind the email.

## Five short paragraphs after the greeting

**1. Preserve the introduction exactly unless Andrew updates the facts:**

I'm a UofT student on co-op in London, UK, working on search, planning and simulation for biotech automation, mostly around failure and recovery in large systems. I've also done a fair bit with RL and MCTS.

**2. First contact and technical connection.** Briefly acknowledge the earlier recipient using their first name, then explain why this second person is relevant. For example, “I also emailed [name] earlier today, but wanted to reach out to you too since…” Name the known first contact, rather than replacing them with “your CTO,” “your colleague,” or “someone on your team.” When two people share a first name, use a natural disambiguation such as “Neil E.” Do not mechanically use a full name. Say “earlier today” only when true on the intended sending date. Never invent a delivery problem, referral, reply or endorsement.

Follow with one concrete technical situation and its plain-English consequence. A natural pattern is “I was reading about [specific work] and got interested in [problem]; [what actually happens].” Do not merely name a research topic or introduce it with “With land-cover mapping, I'd be interested in cases where…” or “The simulation side of my work made me interested in…”. Translate abstract uncertainty into the actual situation: limited driving time means observing one place and missing another; a delayed delivery changes the remaining route; missing a cloud image just before conditions change can affect the forecast. Do not explain the recipient's own field, flatter the company, or use “what stood out,” “particularly drawn to,” “intersection,” or generic “decision-making under uncertainty.”

**3. Connect their problem back to Andrew's work.** Do not reset into generic motivation. Default:

“That problem is close to work I'm already doing, and lately I've been looking for ways to apply more of my [planning / simulation / ML] work to problems with a real-world benefit.”

“That problem” must have a clear antecedent in paragraph 2. The overlap explains why Andrew can contribute; the understated real-world motivation explains why he is writing.

Keep this understated and connected to the technical thought. Preserve genuine distinctive motivation, such as ocean conservation. No statement of purpose, mission flattery, “AI for good,” or invented lifelong interest. This current second-contact preference overrides older advice to remove every motivation sentence.

**4. Small contract ask and one idea.** Default:

“That made me want to ask whether there might be room for a small contract project I could take on. One idea would be [one action, test/comparison, constraint, and useful outcome], but if there’s another [routing / sensor-testing / relevant or simply another] problem the team thinks would matter more in practice, I’d be happy to work on that as well.”

Name the alternative work explicitly. Never use “shape it around,” “tailor it around,” “whatever version of the problem,” or “something along those lines.” Prefer “matter more in practice” to performative impact superlatives.

The idea is a hypothesis, not their established need. It should plausibly fit alongside the co-op. One project sentence, no list of conditions or deliverables, methodology, data-access request, ROI promise, LOI, or mini proposal. Do not make the recipient invent the entire topic; do not pretend Andrew has already designed their project.

**5. Simple call and resume offer.** Default:

“Happy to hop on a call if it sounds worth exploring, and happy to send over my resume as well.”

No attachment by default. Never say the resume is attached. No heavier availability request or generic sales question in place of this close.

## Cadence, subjects and restraint

Sound like a technically capable student writing personally. Ordinary words, contractions, short paragraphs. One longer sentence is fine when it carries one thought. No em dashes, bullets, hype, fake familiarity, or unnecessary colons. Semicolons are allowed naturally. Avoid “leverage,” “drive value,” “compelling,” and polished company hooks.

The natural progression can be “I'm… / I also emailed… / That problem… / That made me… / Happy…”. Do not awkwardly invert sentences or synonym-swap to manufacture variation. Repeated true biography and an explicitly approved close are fine.

Use short concrete technical subjects, such as “Mobile mapping route planning,” “Testing experiment-selection strategies,” or “Routing after delivery delays.” No fake Re:, clickbait, “Quick question,” or self-focused student/AI-for-good pitch.

Usually aim near 155–185 words; over roughly 190, inspect for duplicated problem explanation, overexplained motivation, methodology or company praise. The exact approved examples are longer; this is a soft target, not permission to flatten their cadence or cut necessary context. Remove explanation that teaches the recipient their own field, not the useful technical thought.

## Flow and anti-yap check

Read only the final sentence of each paragraph followed by the first sentence of the next. Does the first actually lead to the second? The technical situation should make “That problem is close to work I'm already doing…” meaningful, which should explain “That made me want to ask…”. If the paragraphs could be rearranged without changing meaning, repair the missing connection.

Do not explain the problem twice. Paragraph 2 explains the situation; paragraph 4 names what Andrew might do about it. For example, missing observations just before conditions change can hurt a forecast; the project replays selected gaps and measures which cause the largest errors. Do not repeat “missing observations make forecasts worse” in both paragraphs.

## Local edit and final check

Preserve the fixed introduction, first-contact identity, sound company-specific idea, and current explicit wording. Repair only actual defects. Verify the paragraph-to-paragraph chain, one understandable technical question, exactly one tentative project, one motivation sentence, openness to the team's needs, and call/resume close. Do not replace the email with a polished pitch.

Check grammar, idiom, facts and voice on the final exact text. A semicolon joins independent clauses; “; especially when…” is a fragment and needs a comma or a full clause. Verify the first contact is named, the motivation follows from the specific problem, the proposal addresses it once, and the alternative explicitly says Andrew can work on another problem. A content edit invalidates previous checks and approval. Recipient verification and scheduling checks remain separate. No claim that a critique, reply, email verification or send occurred without evidence.

Publish requested drafts to the existing Contract CRM records and localhost review page. Keep research and source links in the account detail. Do not alter sent bodies, create duplicate companies, or grant sending eligibility while drafting. Recheck account replies before any separately authorized execution.

When returning an email, give only its subject and email, with the verified To address if requested. Do not explain edits or display internal scoring unless asked.
