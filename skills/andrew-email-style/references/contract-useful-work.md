# Contract outreach: human progression and a clear reason for writing

Andrew's latest September 16 correction supersedes both the earlier exploratory-only direction and the subsequent paid/backlog overcorrection. Use the Neil/FIDO progression and his supplied Archy and Nic drafts as the current references. He is offering himself for contract work, but the email should sound like a technically interested person explaining why he reached out.

## Current opening

For this campaign, preserve Andrew's supplied introduction unless he changes it:

> I'm a UofT student on co-op in London, UK, working on search, planning and simulation for biotech automation, mostly around failure and recovery in large systems. I've also done a fair bit with RL and MCTS.

Refresh co-op and location facts when reusing it in a later context. Do not replace this current supplied wording with isolated resume bullets such as “I've built a digital twin and a layout optimizer” merely to optimize brevity.

## One believable progression

Let one thought cause the next:

who Andrew is → a specific thing he noticed → why it interested him → how it connects to work he already does → why he wants more work of this kind → a small contract ask → one tentative idea → openness to the team's actual needs → a natural call.

This is a reasoning model, not nine sentences or a rigid template. Usually the observation and technical interest belong together, as do the skill connection and motivation. Preserve a strong supplied draft rather than filling slots.

Explain the technical situation in ordinary language. For Axle, an updated price curve arrives after some battery energy has already been used. For Agtonomy, a little less turning space makes a previously feasible plan fail. These situations explain the proposed project and why Andrew is asking this team.

Do not merely insert a product name between a credential and a deliverable. Do not explain the recipient's entire field, narrate a literature review, or duplicate the problem in the project paragraph.

## Motivation that follows from the work

The connection to Andrew's current work makes his interest credible. His broader wish to apply planning, simulation and ML to useful real systems explains why he is looking for a contract.

Use the user's stated motivation, not an invented biography or lifelong passion. Energy, agricultural robotics, environmental monitoring and practical infrastructure can naturally carry a specific real-world consequence. For solver tools and scientific software, wanting to do more difficult planning or simulation work on real systems may be sufficient.

Do not append the same generic real-world-benefit sentence to every company. Preserve genuine motivation and the technical detail that makes it believable; do not remove them for concision or a commercial formula.

## Small contract ask and a tentative idea

“Small contract project” makes the nature of the ask clear. Do not require the word “paid” in every first touch or abruptly insert “I'm looking for a small paid contract outside my co-op.” Use an ordinary transition such as:

> That made me want to ask whether there might be room for a small contract project I could take on.

Follow with one plausible idea that addresses the preceding situation. The idea should be concrete enough to picture and useful enough to discuss: a simulation, comparison, test, implementation or reproducible set of cases. Keep tangible usefulness without turning it into a handover checklist, fixed consulting package or Upwork proposal.

Defer naturally to the team's actual priorities. Andrew's current Archy example explicitly accepts “but I'd much rather work on whatever flexibility-planning problem would actually be useful to the team.” Do not ban that ending or replace it with a compulsory backlog question. Do not ask them to invent the whole engagement: a researched idea comes first.

No assumed code/data access, guaranteed results, invented defects, budget or urgency. If access is unknown, testing an agreed fix may be plausible while promising to implement their internal planner fix is not. Scope, hours, price and duration follow a useful conversation, not a fixed first-touch commitment.

## Natural close

Use the supplied close when it fits:

> Happy to hop on a call if it sounds worth exploring, and happy to send over my resume as well.

Andrew's Archy draft uses “if there might be something worth working on”; preserve it. Do not mechanically add “15 minutes” or “is this on your backlog?” There is no mandatory factual yes/no CTA. No resume attachment by default and no false attachment claim.

Curiosity is allowed. The earlier weakness was repeated vague interest with no clear reason to ask for work, not the existence of phrases such as “I'd be interested,” “worth exploring” or “would be useful.” Diagnose the whole thought instead of applying a word blacklist.

## Targeting, facts and relationship

Keep the better recipient research. Prefer the person close to the relevant work with a plausible path to sponsorship. Engineering leads/directors, scientific owners and hands-on technical founders can all fit. A staff engineer may be a champion without budget authority. Do not mechanically choose a lower title or infer a response rate from headcount.

Verify current employer and address separately from product ownership. A parent-company address can be valid after an acquisition, but unresolved affiliation or responsibility remains a hold. Never use guessed addresses. Follow [buyer targeting](contract-targeting.md).

The Neil/FIDO reference is a second-contact email. Its progression transfers to first touches; its earlier-contact acknowledgment does not. Verify actual history before adding a colleague's name, a date, a referral or any claim of previous contact.
